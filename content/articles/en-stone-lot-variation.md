No two natural stone pieces are completely identical. That natural variation is part of their character, but buyers need a plan to avoid unexpected contrasts across a large facade, floor or wall.

## A small sample is not the whole shipment

A hand sample can indicate colour and finish, but may not show the full range of veins, fossils, pores or shade changes. Ask for a **range of samples** and photographs from multiple slabs, not only the most uniform piece.

## Agree on the visual range

- Define acceptable **lightest and darkest tones**, movement and prominent features.
- Agree whether slabs must be **vein-matched** or **book-matched** for feature walls.
- Request photographs of selected slabs with visible reference IDs.
- Keep approved reference samples for later comparison.
- Specify whether substitutions from another quarry layer or production lot require written approval.

## Create a traceable lot

Before dispatch, a supplier can assign identifiers to slabs and bundles. A simple schedule can include slab number, dimensions, intended room or facade zone, orientation, photograph and packing reference. This makes unloading and installation easier and reduces disputes when a mismatch appears.

## Inspect at the right moment

The best time to identify a visual issue is **before final crating**. Review photos and dimensions, arrange inspection if appropriate and make acceptance criteria clear. Re-cutting or replacing material after arrival can be expensive and slow.

A good purchase brief does not demand that natural stone look manufactured; it defines the acceptable **range of natural variation**.
