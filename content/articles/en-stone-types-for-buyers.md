A stone name on a quotation is not enough to establish suitability. **Composition, finish, intended use and verified test results** matter more than an attractive sample photograph.

## Understand the material families

**Travertine** is a terrestrial limestone that often displays pores, layered tones and a warm architectural character. Filled and unfilled finishes can behave and look differently. It may suit cladding and interior features, subject to the application and the actual stone.

**Marble** is generally a metamorphosed carbonate stone. It offers distinctive veining and takes a high polish, but carbonate stones can be sensitive to acids and surface etching. A beautiful sample is not a guarantee of stain resistance.

**Granite** is commonly chosen where abrasion resistance and durability are important. Trade names are not always the same as strict geological definitions: identify exactly what stone is being offered.

## Start with the application

- **Exterior facade:** request information on anchorage, moisture exposure, weathering, dimensional stability and local code requirements.
- **Wet floors or paving:** assess the *actual surface finish* and slip performance, not only the stone category.
- **Interior feature walls:** visual range, layout and maintenance may lead the decision.
- **Worktops:** confirm cleaning requirements, porosity and stain/etch behavior with representative samples.

## Ask these five questions

1. What is the quarry or commercial stone identification and declared geological type?
2. Are the offered slabs from the same selection or production lot?
3. Which finish and dimensional tolerances are being quoted?
4. Which physical-property test reports are available and applicable to this stone?
5. Will the supplier provide a representative sample and photographs of the actual lot?

**A practical rule:** make the buyer’s specification and intended application part of the inquiry before comparing prices.
